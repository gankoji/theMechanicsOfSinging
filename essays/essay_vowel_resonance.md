# Vowel Tuning, Resonance, and the Science of Unified Barbershop Sound

## Introduction: Why Do Some Sections Sound Like One Voice?

Some barbershop sections sound remarkably unified—like one voice produced by
many singers—while others sound like a collection of individuals. The difference
is not caused by one variable. It emerges from the interaction of vowel targets,
consonant timing, pitch, vocal tone, resonance, dynamics, and listening.

Vowel matching is especially important because vowels occupy most of the audible
duration of a sung phrase. When singers coordinate their vowel targets and
release consonants together, the ensemble’s spectra and diction can become more
consistent. The result is a clearer, more unified sound without requiring singers
to have identical voices or to use identical physical shapes.

This essay explores the acoustic concepts behind vowel matching and resonance,
then applies them to practical coaching. The goal is not to reduce singing to a
formant chart. It is to use acoustic ideas as a way to hear more precisely,
choose useful exercises, and communicate specific adjustments to singers.

## Formants and the Vocal Tract

### What are formants?

A formant is a region of acoustic energy shaped by the resonances of the vocal
tract. The vocal tract acts as a variable acoustic filter: its length, shape, and
constrictions influence which parts of the sound spectrum are emphasized.

The first two formants are particularly useful when discussing vowels:

- **F1** is strongly influenced by vowel openness and by the height of the tongue
  and jaw. More open vowels generally have a higher F1 than close vowels.
- **F2** is strongly influenced by tongue advancement and lip rounding. Front
  vowels generally have a higher F2 than back vowels.

Neither formant is controlled by one articulator alone. Tongue position, jaw
opening, lip shape, vocal-tract length, pharyngeal shaping, and individual
anatomy all contribute. The vowel labels and formant values used in books and
charts are therefore useful descriptions, not universal physical targets for
every singer.

Each vowel has a characteristic spectral pattern. This is why /i/ (“ee”) sounds
different from /u/ (“oo”) even when both are sung at the same fundamental pitch:
the vocal tract emphasizes different regions of the harmonic spectrum.

### Illustrative formant values

The following values are approximate examples, not prescriptions. They are
speaker-, dialect-, and context-dependent, and singing values may differ from
speech values. The IPA symbols should be understood as approximate vowel
categories rather than exact English spelling equivalents.

| Vowel category | Approx. F1 (Hz) | Approx. F2 (Hz) | Broad description |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| /i/ (“ee”) | 270 | 2300 | close front, often unrounded |
| /ɪ/ (“ih”) | 390 | 1990 | near-close front |
| /e/ or /eɪ/ (“ay”) | 530 | 1840 | mid front; often a diphthong in English |
| /æ/ (“a” as in *cat*) | 660 | 1720 | open front |
| /ɑ/ (“ah” as in *father*) | 730 | 1200 | open back |
| /ɔ/ (“aw”) | 570 | 840 | mid back, often rounded |
| /o/ or /oʊ/ (“oh”) | 440 | 1000 | mid back; often a diphthong in English |
| /u/ (“oo”) | 300 | 870 | close back, rounded |

The practical lesson is not that every singer should reproduce these numbers. It
is that changing the vowel changes the distribution of energy in the sound. A
section can therefore lose timbral unity even when its singers are singing the
same written pitch.

## Vowel Matching: A Shared Auditory Target

Vowel matching does not mean that every singer must use the same physical tongue,
jaw, or lip position. Vocal tracts differ, and the same physical adjustment will
not produce exactly the same acoustic result in every person.

The useful goal is a shared auditory target: singers agree on the intended vowel,
listen for a coordinated timbre, and make individual physical adjustments that
help the ensemble approach that sound. When the singers’ vowel spectra become
more similar, the section may sound more unified. When singers use noticeably
different vowel variants, the sound can become diffuse or “mismatched.”

This is why vowel matching is more than pronouncing words the same way. It
includes:

- the vowel selected for the lyric and musical context;
- the degree of openness and rounding;
- the timing of the vowel relative to the consonants;
- the stability of the vowel across pitch and dynamics; and
- the way the vowel interacts with pitch, vibrato, and vocal tone.

A singer may need to modify a vowel across range or dynamic level. The objective
is not to prevent all modification, but to make it purposeful, intelligible, and
coordinated with the rest of the ensemble.

## Resonance and the Barbershop “Ring”

### What is resonance?

In everyday singing language, “resonance” can refer to several related ideas:

- the acoustic filtering and reinforcement produced by the vocal tract;
- the sensation of vibration experienced by the singer; and
- the listener’s perception of richness, clarity, carrying power, or ring.

These ideas overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A sensation in the face
does not by itself prove that a specific acoustic resonance is occurring, and a
measured spectral feature does not necessarily feel the same to every singer.

“Ring” is best treated as a perceptual and musical description of a sound that
is clear, energized, and carrying without sounding forced. It can be supported
by efficient vocal-fold vibration, an appropriate vocal-tract configuration,
coordinated vowels, balanced voices, and a stable pitch relationship. No single
physical sensation or vowel shape guarantees it.

### Resonance is not volume

Resonance is not simply loudness. A quiet sound can be clear and resonant, while
a loud sound can be pressed, diffuse, or poorly coordinated. The practical goal
is efficient sound production: enough energy and clarity for the musical context
without adding unnecessary effort.

Useful contributors may include:

- coordinated breath flow without excessive pressure;
- efficient vocal-fold vibration;
- an appropriately shaped vocal tract;
- a released jaw and tongue that allow the vowel to remain stable;
- coordinated onset and consonant timing; and
- freedom from unnecessary tension.

These are coaching considerations, not diagnoses. A listener can describe the
audible result and propose an experiment without claiming certainty about the
singer’s underlying physiology.

## Migration: Consistent Timbre Across Range and Dynamics

### What is migration?

Barbershop materials use **migration** to describe a tendency for vocal timbre to
change as pitch and volume change—for example, becoming darker at lower pitches
or volumes and brighter at higher pitches or volumes. Some timbral change is
natural and can be expressive. It becomes a problem when it is uncontrolled,
inconsistent among singers, or disruptive to the intended ensemble sound.

For example:

- a bass may become substantially darker on low notes;
- a lead may become noticeably brighter as the melody rises;
- one singer may change timbre more than the others; or
- a whole section may change vowel quality at a different point in the phrase.

### Coordinating migration

The goal is not to eliminate every change in timbre or to impose an artificial
vowel. Instead, singers can work toward:

1. consistent vowel targets across the range;
2. purposeful vowel modification when the register requires it;
3. coordinated resonance and tone among section members;
4. breath and vocal effort that remain appropriate to the phrase; and
5. listening that notices when one singer’s timbre separates from the group.

A useful coaching question is: “What changed in the sound as the pitch or dynamic
changed?” That question is more productive than assuming that one particular
articulator is automatically at fault.

## Consonants, Vowel Duration, and Ensemble Unity

Vowel matching cannot be separated from diction. Consonants carry the text and
coordinate the rhythm, while vowels carry much of the sustained pitch and tone.
A section can have well-matched vowels and still sound unclear if consonants are
late, early, overemphasized, or released inconsistently.

Important ensemble details include:

- entering the vowel together after an initial consonant;
- sustaining the vowel long enough for the chord to speak;
- coordinating diphthong movement rather than allowing singers to change at
  unrelated times;
- keeping voiced consonants from destabilizing the pitch unnecessarily; and
- releasing final consonants together without cutting off the vowel too early.

These details connect diction to resonance and tuning. If one singer closes the
vowel early, the listener hears a different spectrum and a different phrase
shape, even if the written consonant is technically correct.

## Practical Listening and Coaching

### What to listen for

When a section does not sound unified, useful questions include:

1. Are singers using the same intended vowel or noticeably different variants?
2. Does the section retain a coordinated timbre across the register?
3. Do consonants and vowel entrances occur together?
4. Are diphthongs changing at a coordinated point in the phrase?
5. Does the sound become more nasal, dark, bright, spread, or pressed at a
   particular pitch or dynamic?
6. Is the apparent pitch problem actually being intensified by mismatched vowel
   spectra or tone quality?
7. Does the section remain intelligible without sacrificing sustained resonance?

### Common audible issues

| Audible issue | Possible contributors | Coaching direction |
|---|---|---|
| Vowel drift | Changing vowel target as pitch or volume changes | Agree on a target and sustain it through the transition |
| Timbre migration | Uncoordinated darkening or brightening across range | Compare the sound before and after the change; coordinate the adjustment |
| Excessive nasality | Nasal coupling, vowel shape, tongue/jaw configuration | Experiment with a more oral vowel target and listen for changes in clarity |
| Pressed or tense sound | Excessive effort or inefficient coordination | Reduce effort, release unnecessary tension, and seek a freer tone |
| Unclear diction | Consonants or vowel entrances occurring at different times | Isolate the consonant-to-vowel transition and coordinate it |
| Loss of resonance | Vowel constriction, early closure, or changing vocal effort | Sustain the vowel and compare alternative shapes without forcing the sound |

These are possible contributors, not definitive diagnoses. The same audible issue
may have more than one cause, and a useful coach tests a small adjustment while
listening for the result.

### Observation, interpretation, and coaching response

A disciplined coaching process separates what is heard from what is inferred:

- **Observation:** The section loses its unified sound when the phrase rises into
  the upper register.
- **Possible contributors:** Singers may be modifying the vowel differently, or
  one voice may be migrating brighter than the others.
- **Experiment:** Agree on a shared vowel target, sustain it on a single pitch,
  then repeat the phrase while preserving the target through the register change.
- **Evaluation:** Listen for changes in clarity, resonance, intelligibility, and
  ensemble unity. Keep the adjustment that improves the musical result without
  creating strain or distortion.

This process is specific without pretending that a coach can identify an exact
physiological cause from sound alone.

## The APROARTE Connection

Donald Brinegar coined APROARTE and presents the framework in his book
*APROARTE: A Nested Hierarchy of Expressive Singing*. The framework is useful
here as a practical map for discussing vocal production, but it is not a
substitute for listening or for the singer’s individual experience.

The elements most directly connected to vowel matching and resonance are:

- **Audiation:** hearing the intended sound before producing it;
- **Articulators:** coordinating the lips, tongue, jaw, and teeth for clear
  vowels and consonants; and
- **Resonance:** shaping the vocal tract and coordinating the voice for a clear,
  efficient sound.

Posture, respiration, and onset also influence the result. A singer who cannot
maintain a stable breath or onset may not be able to maintain the intended vowel
through the phrase. The framework is therefore most useful when its elements are
considered as interacting parts of one vocal system.

## Exercises for Vowel Matching

The following exercises are drawn from or adapted from NWS materials:

1. **Vowel sequence on one pitch:** Move through a sequence such as “ee–eh–ah–
   oh–oo” while keeping the jaw released and noticing how the sound changes.
2. **Consonant-to-vowel clarity:** Practice short consonants followed by a stable
   vowel, such as “pa-pa-pa,” “ta-ta-ta,” and “ka-ka-ka.” Then coordinate the
   exercise as a section.
3. **Ng-to-vowel glide:** Sustain an “ng” hum, then open gradually into a vowel
   while observing whether the sense of clarity carries into the vowel. Treat the
   buzzing sensation as feedback, not proof of a particular acoustic mechanism.
4. **Shared vowel target:** Have singers agree on a vowel target, sustain it on a
   single pitch, and then transfer it to the song passage.
5. **Diphthong coordination:** Sustain the first element of a diphthong and move
   to the second element together. Listen for whether the word remains clear and
   the ensemble remains unified.
6. **Register and migration exercise:** Sing the same vowel through a comfortable
   ascending and descending pattern. Notice where each singer’s timbre changes,
   then experiment with coordinated, non-forced adjustments.

## Conclusion

Vowel tuning and resonance are practical tools for developing ensemble unity. The
acoustic concepts of formants and vocal-tract filtering help explain why vowel
choices affect timbre, clarity, and perceived blend. They do not prescribe one
physical shape for every singer.

Effective coaching begins with listening: identify what changes, describe the
audible result, test a small and purposeful adjustment, and evaluate whether the
ensemble becomes clearer, more resonant, more intelligible, and more unified.
The science serves the art when it helps singers make coordinated musical choices
without forcing the voice or reducing expressive singing to a set of numbers.
