# APROARTE: A Nested Hierarchy for Expressive Singing

## Introduction

Effective vocal pedagogy needs both a useful map and a willingness to listen beyond
the map. **APROARTE** is a framework coined by Donald Brinegar and presented in
his book *APROARTE: A Nested Hierarchy of Expressive Singing*. The framework
organizes vocal preparation from internal musical intention through the physical
systems that support expressive sound.

The word “hierarchy” is important, but it should not be read as a rigid checklist
in which one element is permanently finished before the next begins. Singing is
an interacting system. Posture affects respiration; respiration affects onset;
onset affects tone; articulators affect resonance; and expression continually
shapes the singer’s choices. The framework is most useful as a way to identify
where a problem may be entering the system and to choose a manageable experiment.

This essay applies the framework to barbershop singing and coaching. It treats
APROARTE as Brinegar’s framework, not as an original system of mine.

## The APROARTE Elements

### A — Audiation

**Audiation** is the ability to hear music internally: a pitch, melody, rhythm,
chord, or expressive intention before and while producing it.

Audiation gives the voice an internal target. In ensemble singing, that target
may include both an individual part and the larger chord. A singer who hears
only the written note may have difficulty adjusting that note in relation to the
ensemble. A singer who hears the chord’s function and desired color has more
information available for a coordinated entrance and sustained tone.

Useful audiation practices include:

- sing a pitch, stop, hold it internally, and resume it;
- hear a part internally while another part is sounding;
- imagine the complete chord before singing one chord tone;
- speak or silently conduct a phrase while hearing its rhythm and shape; and
- identify the intended vowel, tone, and expressive direction before phonating.

Audiation is not the same as perfect pitch. It is a trainable relationship between
musical imagination, attention, and vocal action.

### P — Posture

**Posture** is the body’s organization against gravity while standing or sitting
for singing. The goal is not to create a rigid “singing position,” but to provide
a stable and adaptable foundation for movement and breath.

Useful principles include:

- balanced weight over the feet;
- knees that are released rather than locked;
- a long, mobile spine;
- a head and neck that are free to move;
- shoulders and arms that are not held artificially; and
- a jaw that can release without the rest of the body collapsing.

Posture affects the conditions in which breathing, phonation, and articulation
occur. It does not guarantee a good sound by itself. A singer can appear aligned
and still be tense, while another singer may move naturally and produce an
efficient tone.

In a chorus, posture also communicates readiness and supports visual unity. The
physical goal remains freedom and adaptability rather than uniform posing.

### R — Respiration

**Respiration** supplies the airflow and pressure changes that support phonation.
Singing requires coordination among the respiratory muscles, vocal folds, and
vocal tract; it is not simply a matter of taking the largest possible breath.

Useful breathing principles include:

- inhale enough for the phrase rather than habitually overfilling;
- allow the ribs and torso to remain available for movement;
- regulate airflow rather than releasing it all at the beginning of the phrase;
- coordinate breath with musical phrasing; and
- avoid substituting visible abdominal motion for an actual listening-based
  assessment of the sound.

“Support” is a useful teaching word, but it can mean different things to
 different singers. It should lead to a freer, more stable, and more responsive
sound—not to unnecessary muscular gripping or pressure.

In barbershop, breath management affects sustained chords, dynamic changes,
phrase length, and the ability to maintain a unified vowel. Chorus breathing
also has an ensemble dimension: staggered breathing can preserve continuity, but
it must not interrupt the group’s diction or musical shape.

### O — Onset

**Onset** is the coordination at the beginning of phonation. A singer brings
together airflow, vocal-fold vibration, vowel shape, pitch intention, and the
musical moment of the entrance.

A useful onset is clear and coordinated without being breathy, delayed, or
pressed. Different musical contexts may call for different qualities of onset:

- a gentle onset may suit a soft or intimate entrance;
- a clean, energized onset may support a precise ensemble entrance; and
- a more emphatic onset may be expressive when it remains free rather than
  forced.

It is too simple to describe one onset type as ideal for every situation. The
important questions are whether the sound begins when intended, whether the
vocal folds and breath are coordinated, and whether the entrance supports the
pitch, vowel, diction, and expression of the phrase.

In a quartet or chorus, onset coordination also includes consonant timing. A
precise vowel entrance can be undermined if the initial consonants arrive at
slightly different moments.

### A — Articulators

**Articulators** shape vowels and consonants. They include the lips, tongue,
jaw, teeth, and related structures that influence the sound’s acoustic shape and
its intelligibility.

Vowels carry much of the sustained tone, while consonants carry information and
help coordinate rhythm. Effective articulation therefore requires both clarity
and continuity:

- the vowel must remain available long enough for the tone and chord to develop;
- consonants must communicate the text without unnecessarily interrupting the
  sound;
- diphthongs should be coordinated rather than changed independently by each
  singer; and
- the jaw and tongue should remain responsive rather than fixed or excessively
  tense.

A chorus does not need identical physical articulator positions. Singers have
different anatomies. The practical goal is a shared audible vowel and coordinated
diction, achieved through whatever individual adjustments produce that result.

### R — Resonance

**Resonance** describes the way the vocal tract filters and reinforces the sound
produced by the vocal folds. Changes in the tongue, jaw, lips, pharyngeal space,
and other parts of the tract influence the distribution of energy across the
spectrum.

Resonance is often discussed through sensations such as “placement,” “forward
ring,” or “mask resonance.” These sensations can be useful feedback, but they are
not direct measurements of the acoustic event. A vibration felt in the face does
not prove that the sound is resonating in a literal facial cavity.

In ensemble singing, coordinated resonance can contribute to:

- a more unified timbre;
- clearer vowels;
- efficient projection;
- a stable relationship among voices; and
- the perceptual impression of “ring.”

Resonance is not the same as volume. A loud sound may be strained or diffuse,
while a quiet sound may be clear and efficient. The useful experiment is to find
the vocal-tract configuration that improves clarity and carrying power without
forcing the voice or distorting the vowel.

### T — Tone

**Tone** is the sound’s overall vocal quality: its clarity, warmth, brightness,
energy, density, and freedom. Tone results from the interaction of vocal-fold
vibration, breath, vocal-tract shaping, vowel, register, dynamics, and musical
intention.

Tone should not be treated as a fixed personal property that must be erased in
favor of one standardized sound. Ensemble unity does not require every singer to
have the same voice. It requires singers to coordinate the aspects of tone that
matter for the musical context.

Useful questions include:

- Is the tone free or pressed?
- Is it clear enough for the text and musical texture?
- Does it remain stable through the range and dynamics?
- Does the singer’s individual tone support or obscure the ensemble sound?
- Does the tone communicate the phrase rather than merely produce a note?

### E — Expression

**Expression** is the musical and emotional communication carried by the sound.
It includes phrasing, timing, dynamics, lyric meaning, vowel color, articulation,
energy, and the relationship between musical intention and vocal action.

Expression is not an optional decoration added after technique. Technique creates
options; expression determines how those options serve the music. At the same
time, expressive choices should remain connected to healthy, coordinated vocal
production. A singer should not create emotional intensity by habitually forcing
the tone or sacrificing intelligibility.

In barbershop, expression is an ensemble responsibility. Singers coordinate
breath, vowel, consonant, timing, dynamics, and body language so that the musical
idea reaches the listener as one coherent performance.

## The Nested Hierarchy in Practice

The value of APROARTE is not simply that it supplies eight memorable labels. Its
value is that it encourages a singer or coach to consider how one layer affects
the others.

For example, suppose a section sounds unified on a sustained chord but loses
pitch stability as the phrase rises. A useful investigation might ask:

1. **Audiation:** Do the singers hear the target pitch and vowel before the
   transition?
2. **Posture:** Can they remain physically free as the phrase rises?
3. **Respiration:** Is the airflow sufficient and appropriately regulated?
4. **Onset:** Did the pitch and vowel begin with coordinated timing?
5. **Articulators:** Did the vowel or diphthong change inconsistently?
6. **Resonance:** Did the vocal-tract shape create a less stable or less unified
   spectrum?
7. **Tone:** Did the sound become pressed, breathy, dark, or overly bright?
8. **Expression:** Is the musical intention causing a useful color change, or is
   an uncontrolled technical change being mistaken for expression?

This is not a diagnostic checklist that proves a single cause. It is a sequence
of questions that can help narrow the next coaching experiment.

## A Coaching Process Based on APROARTE

A practical session might follow this sequence:

1. **Describe the desired result.** Agree on the pitch, vowel, tone, diction, and
   expressive intention.
2. **Listen to the passage.** Identify the earliest audible point at which the
   result changes.
3. **Choose one likely layer.** Avoid changing posture, breath, vowel, onset, and
   resonance simultaneously.
4. **Run a small experiment.** Isolate the pitch, vowel, consonant, or phrase and
   repeat it with one intentional adjustment.
5. **Listen again.** Keep the adjustment only if it improves the musical result
   without creating strain or distortion.
6. **Reconnect the exercise to the phrase.** A successful isolated drill matters
   only if it transfers to the actual music.

This process protects singers from vague instructions such as “make it more
resonant” or “support more.” It also protects coaches from overdiagnosing a
physiological cause that cannot be established from listening alone.

## Conclusion

APROARTE, as developed by Donald Brinegar, provides a nested way to think about
singing: musical intention depends on internal hearing, physical organization,
breath, onset, articulation, resonance, tone, and expression working together.
The elements are distinguishable, but they are not independent.

For barbershop singers and coaches, the framework is useful because it connects
technical choices to the unified sound of the ensemble. It encourages specific
listening, small experiments, and a return to the musical phrase. Its greatest
value is not in assigning blame to one part of the mechanism, but in helping
singers discover which coordinated change allows the music to become clearer,
freer, and more expressive.
