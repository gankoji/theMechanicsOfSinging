# Just Intonation, Flexible Tuning, and the Acoustics of Barbershop “Lock and Ring”

## Introduction: Why Singers Can Adjust Beyond Equal Temperament

One of the most useful concepts for a barbershop singer is that the pitch of a
voice is not fixed in the way the pitch of a piano key is fixed. A piano uses
twelve-tone equal temperament (12-TET), a practical tuning system that divides
the octave into twelve equal semitones. This compromise allows a fixed-pitch
instrument to play in every key and to modulate without retuning.

Singers have a different capability. We can adjust pitch continuously while
performing. That flexibility allows a quartet or chorus to tune intervals in
relation to the evolving chord rather than adhering rigidly to an equal-tempered
reference. The result can be a sound that is unusually stable, resonant, and
unified—the sensation barbershoppers describe as “lock and ring.”

This does not mean that 12-TET is “wrong,” that every interval should be tuned
to one universal ratio, or that a locked chord contains no audible beating.
Rather, just intonation and harmonic acoustics provide useful models for
understanding why responsive, relational tuning can improve an ensemble’s sound.

## The Acoustics: What Happens When a Chord Locks?

### Harmonic alignment

A sung tone contains a fundamental frequency and a series of partials, commonly
called overtones. The frequencies and relative strengths of those partials
contribute to the tone’s color. When the partials of different voices align
favorably, the combined sound can become more stable, resonant, and unified.

One way to describe some of these relationships is through simple frequency
ratios. For example, a justly tuned octave has a 2:1 ratio, and a justly tuned
perfect fifth has a 3:2 ratio. These ratios are useful reference points, but they
are not a complete recipe for every chord. The appropriate pitch relationship
depends on harmonic function, voice part, register, melodic direction, vowel,
vibrato, dynamics, and the surrounding progression.

The following table compares several just-intonation relationships with their
equal-tempered counterparts. The final column means “12-TET interval minus the
listed just-intonation interval”; a positive number means the 12-TET interval is
higher, and a negative number means it is lower.

| Interval | Just-intonation ratio | Approximate 12-TET difference |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unison | 1:1 | 0 cents |
| Octave | 2:1 | 0 cents |
| Perfect fifth | 3:2 | −2 cents |
| Perfect fourth | 4:3 | +2 cents |
| Major third | 5:4 | +14 cents |
| Harmonic/septimal seventh | 7:4 | +31 cents |

A cent is a logarithmic unit describing a pitch ratio; it is not the same thing
as a hertz, which describes an absolute frequency. The values above are
approximations. The intervals are shown as ascending relationships above a
common reference or root. The table is an illustration, not a complete tuning
system for every musical context. The 7:4 relationship is often called a
harmonic or septimal seventh; it is not a universal tuning prescription for
every interval labeled “minor seventh.”

### Beats, roughness, and resonance

When nearby frequencies interact, listeners may hear a periodic fluctuation or
“beating.” As a simplified description, the beat rate corresponds to the
frequency difference between components; spectral content, amplitude, register,
and the interaction of multiple partials also affect perceived roughness.
Reducing unwanted beating can make a sustained interval feel more settled, but
“lock and ring” is not reducible to the total absence of beats. Vibrato, timbre,
register, dynamics, room acoustics, and the balance among voices all affect what
we hear.

The practical result of favorable alignment may include:

- reduced roughness in sustained intervals;
- clearer harmonic definition;
- greater perceived resonance and projection;
- a more unified ensemble timbre; and
- the impression that the sound has expanded beyond the individual voices.

Combination tones and other auditory effects may also contribute to the
experience of a rich chord, but their audibility and musical importance vary
with frequency, level, spectrum, and listener. Singers and coaches should attend
to the audible result rather than assume that a particular sensation proves that
a specific mathematical ratio has been achieved.

## Why Barbershop Singers May Depart from 12-TET

In 12-TET, each semitone has the same frequency ratio, approximately
$2^{1/12}$, or 1.0595. This makes the octave mathematically uniform, but it
means that most intervals do not exactly match simple whole-number ratios.
For example:

- the equal-tempered perfect fifth is slightly narrower than 3:2;
- the equal-tempered perfect fourth is slightly wider than 4:3; and
- the equal-tempered major third is substantially wider than 5:4.

A fixed-pitch instrument must accept these compromises in order to work in all
keys. A vocal ensemble is not limited in the same way. Singers can adjust their
pitches in response to the tonal center, the chord, and one another. That
flexibility is a resource, not a command to tune every note to a single fixed
just-intonation chart.

Equal temperament remains useful in barbershop. It can establish a key, provide
a learning reference, support accompaniment, and help singers locate written
pitches. The important distinction is that singers can treat the reference as a
starting point and then listen responsively to the chord.

## How Singers Adjust in Real Time

### Melodic and harmonic priorities

A singer’s pitch choice may need to serve more than one musical purpose. A
melodic line may need a clear tonal direction and expressive shape. A sustained
chord tone may need to settle into a relationship with the other voices. A singer
moving between those roles may make small adjustments as the harmony changes.

It is therefore too categorical to say that leads always use Pythagorean tuning,
that harmony parts always use pure just intonation, or that the bass always tunes
lower. Those ideas can describe tendencies in particular musical situations,
but the appropriate adjustment depends on the chord, the voice part, the
register, the musical line, and the ensemble’s sound.

The bass often has an important role in establishing the tonal center, while the
lead carries much of the melody’s identity. The harmony parts complete the chord
and help define its color. All four parts, however, must listen and adjust. No
part is exempt from responsibility for the combined sound.

### Why precise tuning is difficult

Singers do not usually improve intonation by memorizing cents values and
reproducing them mechanically. They develop a coordinated auditory and motor
skill. Useful components include:

- **Audiation:** hearing the intended pitch or chord relationship internally
  before and during singing.
- **Beat sensitivity:** noticing changes in roughness and stability as pitches
  approach or move away from one another.
- **Tonal-center awareness:** knowing how an individual note functions within the
  key and the current chord.
- **Vocal coordination:** adjusting pitch without introducing unnecessary
  tension, breathiness, or instability.
- **Repetition:** practicing the sound and sensation of successful tuning until
  the response becomes more reliable.

The goal is not to make singers chase each other. Each singer needs enough
independent audiation and tonal awareness to contribute a confident pitch while
remaining responsive to the ensemble.

## Listening and Coaching Applications

Understanding acoustics can sharpen a singer’s and coach’s listening without
reducing musical decisions to theoretical ratios. Useful questions include:

1. **Pitch stability:** Do sustained notes and chords remain centered, or do they
   drift?
2. **Chord alignment:** Do the voices sound settled and mutually supportive, or
   does the sound remain rough and disconnected?
3. **Tonal-center continuity:** Does the ensemble preserve the key through
   entrances, releases, transitions, and dynamic changes?
4. **Relational listening:** Do singers adjust to the chord without audibly
   chasing one another?
5. **Interaction with vowels and tone:** Do vowel shape, resonance, vibrato, or
   vocal tension obscure otherwise accurate pitch relationships?
6. **Musical integration:** Does the ensemble maintain tuning while also
   communicating phrasing, lyric meaning, and expression?

It is also useful to distinguish between a local tuning issue and a larger
performance pattern. One unstable entrance may have a different cause and
solution from an ensemble that gradually rises or falls throughout a song.

### Observation, interpretation, and coaching

A useful coaching habit is to separate what is heard from what is inferred:

- **Observation:** The sustained major third becomes increasingly rough during
  the held chord.
- **Interpretation:** The interval is not remaining stable in relation to the
  chord’s tonal center; vowel or tone changes may also be affecting the
  perceived alignment.
- **Coaching response:** Identify the specific moment, describe the audible
  effect, and suggest that the singers stabilize the vowel and listen for a more
  settled relationship rather than attempting to force a particular cents value.

This approach keeps feedback specific without pretending to identify a singer’s
exact physiological cause from sound alone.

## Practical Exercises for Developing Intonation

The following exercises are drawn from the NWS curriculum or adapted for this
essay; they are consistent with the broader principles above:

1. **Drone-based tuning:** Sing against a pitch pipe or drone. Match the
   reference, then deliberately move slightly above and below it to hear and
   feel changes in the beat rate before returning to center.
2. **Partial singing:** Sing only two or three parts of a chord, such as lead and
   bass, to focus attention on a particular interval before adding the remaining
   voices.
3. **Slowed-down recordings:** Sing with a slowed recording and focus on the
   relationship among voices, the stability of sustained chords, and the effect
   of releases and transitions.
4. **Duet tuning:** Have two singers work on a passage together before adding
   the other parts. This can reveal whether the issue is local to an interval or
   emerges only when the full chord is present.
5. **Vowel stabilization:** Sustain a difficult chord while keeping the vowel
   shape consistent. Compare the sound before and after the vowel is clarified;
   this helps distinguish pitch problems from resonance and diction problems.

These exercises should develop listening and coordination, not encourage singers
to force the voice or pursue a visual tuner at the expense of musical
responsiveness.

## Conclusion

Just intonation and harmonic acoustics help explain why flexible, relational
intonation can support the barbershop sound. But “lock and ring” is not the
mechanical application of one ratio chart, nor is it defined by eliminating every
beat. It is an audible result of singers who maintain a tonal center, adjust
responsively, coordinate vowels and tone, and preserve unity through musical
change.

For a singer or coach, this understanding supports disciplined listening:
identify what is unstable, describe its audible effect, distinguish pitch from
related vowel or tone issues, and give feedback that is specific and musically
useful.
