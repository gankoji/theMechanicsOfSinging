1. Introduction

Goal: Every musical choice connects to one of four themes: Melody, Lyric, Harmony, Rhythm.
Think of these as the pillars of expressive singing and interpretation.

Musicality is simpler than it seems. Almost all musical choices come down to three core elements:

  • Dynamics - The volume and intensity of your sound
  • Timing - When and how you deliver notes and phrases
  • Embellishments - The decorative touches that add personality

The musical themes help you pick a cohesive set of choices for these three elements. Each theme has specific hallmarks that indicate when it's called for, and each creates a distinct impact on your audience.

Key Insight: Multiple themes can be at play simultaneously in a song, but you usually want one theme to win out for a cohesive performance.

2. Thematic Deep Dives

Melody

What is melody? Melody is a linear succession of musical tones perceived as a single entity. It combines pitch and rhythm, and is often the most memorable part of a song. Melodies give music its identity and emotional shape.

  • Draw phrase shapes/contours to visualize the rise and fall of the tune.
  • Plan breath placement at peaks and turnarounds for phrasing.
  • Listen for motifs and repetition; these help anchor the melody in memory.

    • Dynamics

    Generally, follow the melodic line, but viewed at the phrase level. For example, "but if someone like you" is all quieter than "found someone like me" even though "like you" and "like me" are literally the same note. Bring out the moving line.

    Timing

    Try not to interrupt the melody line with a breath. Bad example: "Take me out to the" (breath) "ball game".

    Embellishments

    Counter-melodies, clearly intentional breaks from normal melody (the moon that lingered over "foggy London town"), key changes. Beware "just change the melody note" as a solution to musical difficulties.

    Hallmarks

    A clear and interesting melody line, usually tied to the narrative of a hero/villain.

    Audience Impact

    Gets stuck in your head, makes the audience think you're better than you are because they embellish themselves.

    Examples

    • Both Sides Now (The Idea of North)
    • On the Sunny Side of the Street (Nikki Yanofsky)
    • The Vocal Majority • Bridge Over Fire and Rain • 2025 International Chorus Contest

Dynamics

Totally story based, emphasize adjectives and repetitions, and highlight the literary arc. Bring out the conflict and resolution.

Timing

As spoken (it's harder than it seems).

Embellishments

Repetitions, clever rhymes, harmony saying things while the lead holds notes.

Hallmarks

Lots of homophony, lots of descriptions "the shimmering leaves of the mulberry tree whisper a serenade above", and declarations "Heart of my heart, I LOVE YOU".

Audience Impact

Words can connect well, often draws out similar experiences so an audience lives their own parallel story. Can be cathartic.

Examples

  • Midtown • Still Hurting (from The Last Five Years) • arr. Theo Hicks • 2023 Quartet Semifinals
  • Children Will Listen (Bernadette Peters)
  • You Turned The Tables On Me (Anita O'Day)
Actionable Tips:
  • Mark phrase shapes in your score.
  • Practice singing with varied dynamics to shape the melody.
  • Try singing the opening phrase of "The Star-Spangled Banner" and notice its contour.
Demo: Call & response: Sing the opening phrase of “The Star-Spangled Banner” together, focusing on phrase shape.

Lyric

Why are lyrics important? Lyrics tell the story and drive the emotional content of a song. Diction and word emphasis shape how the message is received.

  • Use diction choices for nuance: experiment with vowel shapes and consonant clarity.
  • Emphasize key words to highlight the story.
  • Vary your delivery to match the song’s mood and meaning.

    • Dynamics

    It's all about suspension/release. Bring out every additional color note.

    Timing

    Can go slower, MUST have wall of sound, make sure each chord sounds before moving on (venue specific).

    Embellishments

    Tritone substitution, dissonance, ninth chords. Arpeggiation, duets/trios.

    Hallmarks

    Orchestral introduction/interludes. Depends on oos and swiping without changing words a lot.

    Audience Impact

    Paints a picture or sets a scene where the song can live. Developments can be independent of the melody or harmony, making foreshadowing possible.

    Examples

    • Sing Sing Sing (New York Voices)
    • The Nearness Of You - Highline
    • Westminster Chorus - I'll Be Here [from The Wild Party]

Dynamics

Quieter than anyone ever wants to do it. Live/die by accents.

Timing

Stay where you start, groove transcends tempo.

Embellishments

Mess with timing, not notes. Syncopation, pickups, repeats, articulations, accents. Lead diverges from the rhythm, while the group continues it.

Hallmarks

Not rubato to start with, but more, the song derives its power from the rhythm (We will rock you).

Audience Impact

Makes you want to dance and engage with the performers. Easy to sing along with.

Examples

  • A Thousand Years (Christina Perri) (good contrast from Harmony)
  • No One is Alone (Cyrille Aimee) (good contrast from Lyric)
  • Three and a Half Men • Ease On Down The Road/Brand New Day • BHS 2024 International Quartet Contest
Actionable Tips:
  • Use a vowel emphasis chart to practice clarity.
  • Try speaking or singing a simple poem, varying diction for interpretation.
  • Mark lyric keywords in your score for emphasis.
Exercise: Speak or sing a short poem, experimenting with diction and emphasis for different interpretations.

Harmony

What is harmony? Harmony is the combination of different musical notes played or sung simultaneously to produce chords and chord progressions. It adds depth, color, and emotional tension or release to music.

  • Listen to your “neighbor” part and match vowel and timbre for blend.
  • Tune 3rds and 5ths carefully; use a drone for sectional tuning.
  • Understand how chords move and resolve for expressive phrasing.
Actionable Tips:
  • Practice singing over a drone (I–IV–V–I) to tune intervals.
  • Blend with your section by matching vowel shapes and dynamics.
  • Notice how tension and release in harmony affect the mood.
Demo: Director drones I–IV–V–I; singers blend and tune their parts.

Rhythm

What is rhythm? Rhythm is the pattern of sounds and silences in music, creating pulse, groove, and movement. It includes beat, tempo, and syncopation.

  • Mark strong beats and ties in your music.
  • Practice subdivision tapping before singing.
  • Experiment with rubato (flexible timing) for expressive phrasing.
Actionable Tips:
  • Clap and sing a syncopated barbershop tag together.
  • Use a metronome or tap your foot to internalize the pulse.
  • Try holding ties and marking subdivisions in your score.
Demo: Clap and sing a syncopated tag as a group.

3. Recap & Q&A

Key Takeaways:
  • Every musical choice can be traced to melody, lyric, harmony, or rhythm.
  • Expressive singing comes from understanding and balancing these elements.
  • Practice actionable tips for each theme to improve as a musician.

5. Ongoing Suggestions

  • Weekly warm-ups: Vowel-matching on chords, rhythm clapping patterns
  • Repertoire annotations: Mark melody jumps & lyric keywords in scores
  • Minute drills: Quick echo-phrases (melody), one-word emphasis (lyrics)
  • Recording & feedback: Assign one member per theme to note observations each week
